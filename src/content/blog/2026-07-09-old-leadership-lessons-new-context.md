<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Ron Wallace started as a UPS driver in rural Idaho in 1966 and retired 36 years later as President of UPS International, running operations in 200+ countries. His book, "Leadership Lessons from a UPS Driver," turns that climb into a handful of blunt, unglamorous principles. Here are four insights that stayed with me -</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>1. Best 5 vs 5 best.</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Five average players who move as a unit beat five stars who don't. With AI blurring roles - PMs writing code, engineers writing specs - how well a team moves together matters more than any one person's skill ceiling.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>2. Manage yourself before you manage others.</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You can't ask for discipline you don't have. When everyone has an agent doing part of their job, self-management - what to delegate, what to check, what to own - is the whole game.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>3. 10% planning, 90% execution.</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That ratio assumed execution was the bottleneck. Agents and other tools now handle much of the execution. Does the split flip? Not sure - bad planning just produces fast, wrong output at scale. But the balance is shifting, and finding the new ratio is one of the more interesting problems before us.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>4. Change is inevitable; growth is optional.</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>UPS survived by repeatedly outgrowing its own identity. Feels like the line of the year - nobody's job description is stable, and adapting is a choice, not a default.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
