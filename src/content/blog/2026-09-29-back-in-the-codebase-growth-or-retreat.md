<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>LeadDev's 2026 numbers are stark: engineering managers doing hands-on coding jumped from 20% to 35% in a year. Tech leads, EMs, managers of managers, CTOs - every layer is spending more time in code and reviews than it did in 2025. The framing is a celebration: the "player-coach" model Gergely Orosz predicted back in 2024 has finally arrived, AI knocked down the barrier, and leaders get to be technical again.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I don't think it's that simple, and I don't think the data even agrees with itself.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Start with Gregor Ojstersek, who's tracking the same underlying shift - managers moving back toward IC-shaped work - and reaches a different verdict. His read is that management skills (delegating, breaking work down, giving feedback, iterating on output) are exactly the skills AI-assisted engineering rewards, which is why managers are good at this. But he's explicit that doing the work yourself instead of delegating is a bottleneck risk to avoid at all costs. And he treats "going IC again" as a different track, not something you layer on top of still running a team. That's a quiet rebuttal of the player-coach story: same data, opposite conclusion about whether more hands-on time is good news.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Then there's Jellyfish's read on where 2026 leadership value actually sits, and it isn't in anyone's personal commit history. Their State of Engineering Management report found that only 46% of orgs are tracking AI-specific metrics at all, even though 84% call productivity a board-level priority - and that rising token cost, not security or quality, is now the number one adoption concern. If the leadership premium in 2026 is measurement discipline - proving where AI spend turns into outcomes - then a manager's personal coding hours are exactly the kind of activity that feels productive without moving the number anyone's actually being judged on.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Even LeadDev's own article half-concedes this. Emma Bostian, quoted in the piece celebrating the shift, says the impact comes from empowering the team, not from increasing output through your own coding. That's not a footnote - that's the article disagreeing with its own headline.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>So where does that leave the manager reading this and wondering what to do with their Tuesday afternoon?</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I think the honest answer is that it's not a universal question, and treating it like one is where all three of these pieces go slightly wrong. It's partly personal style - some leaders think better with their hands in the code and are worse at everything else if you take that away from them; others were never going to be good reviewers-of-taste and are better used entirely on the people and roadmap side. And it's partly organizational culture - a company that still equates "credible leader" with "ships code" will reward the hours LeadDev is measuring, regardless of what Jellyfish's data says the board actually cares about. A company that's built real AI-accountability muscle will notice pretty quickly if a manager's hands-on hours aren't showing up anywhere in outcomes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Which means the question worth asking isn't "should managers code more." It's whether your own answer to that question is coming from what actually makes you effective, or from what your organization happens to reward right now - because those two things are not guaranteed to point the same direction, and this year's data makes a decent case that for a lot of people, they don't.</p>
