<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This month marks twenty years since I started my bachelor's degree, and I've been thinking about what stayed with me.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I studied CS alongside Amirim, the humanities excellence program at HUJI. It turned out to be exactly the right mix. The humanities side taught me to read closely, write clearly, and question a text rather than just absorb it. It introduced me to ideas and cultures I might never have encountered otherwise. I use those skills throughout my work, perhaps more than ever now. Writing a prompt that conveys what you actually want, or reading an agent's output critically enough to trust it, draws on the same muscles.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That wasn't obvious to me at the time. In the second semester of my first year, I wanted out. I was ready to switch to economics and sociology. Prof. Dorit Aharonov gave me advice I've kept: I was already in the middle of the semester, so I should write down the decision, finish the semester, and come back to it. I only did the writing-down part metaphorically, but I still try to follow the principle. If I don't have to decide right now, I give myself time to cool off and see the question from a wider angle.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What stayed with me wasn't only the material. Some of the friends I made in that program are still the people whose judgment I trust most, whether I'm weighing a career decision or sharing a draft. A degree puts you in a room with people who can shape how you think for decades. That rarely appears on the syllabus.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Would I study it again? Probably. I can't picture myself doing anything else, and it led me to work I love. Can someone get where they're going without a degree? Of course. A degree is a signal, and it has a cost. Whether you can do without it depends on other opportunities too - in Israel, for example, service in an elite tech unit can open doors that aren't equally open to everyone.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>But the content of the degree needs to change, probably more than we're admitting. Even if a CS degree is meant to prepare people for research rather than a particular job, parts of the syllabus reflect constraints that have shifted. In my database course, we spent two thirds of the semester optimizing schemas because storage was expensive. Today storage is much cheaper. Now another shift is happening much faster: boilerplate, syntax, and even a first pass at a schema, work that used to take time, can be produced by a model in seconds. That doesn't make the work trivial. It changes where the hard part is.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Judgment, taste, knowing which question to ask, and reading someone else's output - human or agent - closely enough to accept or reject it: these matter more as the mechanics get easier. I'd spend less time teaching mechanics in isolation and more time on those habits of mind. The humanities side of my degree gave me a head start on them, almost by accident.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I've always loved that this profession never sits still. The last four years, and especially the last two, have forced me to rethink what is expensive, what is automatic, and what a junior engineer's day even looks like. That can be exhausting. It's also why I still want to do this work.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Twenty years ago, I couldn't have described the job I have today. I doubt someone starting a CS degree now can describe the job they'll have in twenty years either. That's fine. Most of the specific tools I learned are gone or changed beyond recognition. What stayed is how I learned to think about problems, and who I learned it alongside. If a degree still offers that, it has a future worth defending - and updating.</p>
